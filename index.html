Teaching notes
  • Why numerals. A numeral names a chord by its office in the key, not its letter. I–V–vi–IV is one shape in every key — so a song in G and a song in D become the same lesson.
  • Reading the plate. Capitals are major, lowercase minor, lowercase with ° diminished. Stroke weight follows: heavy major, fine minor, broken diminished. Height rises with the degree.
  • The minor keys use the natural minor — i, ii°, III, iv, v, VI, VII. A minor is built from exactly the notes of C major, which is the point worth making: same seven notes, different home. Most worship charts in a minor key raise the v to a major V for the final cadence; sound that from Outside the key.
  • Outside the key. Any of the twelve roots, major or minor, labelled with its numeral relative to the current tonic — bVII, #IV, V of a minor key, and so on. Use it to show why a borrowed chord feels like a door opening.
  • Tempo and click. Set a slow tempo, turn the click on, and let a trainee play the progression along with it. That is Week 3 in a single screen. Each chord lasts two beats.
  • Start here. Hide the names, play degrees 1, 4 and 5 only, and let them hear home / away / tension before the minors are introduced.
  • The transfer test. Once C is secure, change the key to G and repeat. If the numerals still hold, the idea has landed.
  • On a phone. Add it to the homescreen and it opens like an app and works with no signal. On iPhone the first tap unlocks audio, and the silent switch must be off.
